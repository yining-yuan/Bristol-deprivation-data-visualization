A tale of two cities

Persistent educational and economic disparities exist within Bristol

Stark within-city disparities can be seen in the number of adults achieving Level 4+ qualifications in Bristol's lowest (Redland) and highest (Hartcliffe and Withywood) areas of deprivation.

Adults with a Level 4+ qualification, by ward

In the affluent ward of Redland, it is rare for a child to live in a low-income household; however, in Lawrence Hill, the city's most deprived ward, more than half of children are growing up in relative poverty. Across Bristol as a whole, a quarter of children live in low-income homes after housing costs.

Qualification levels are high but transport and housing issues shape deprivation in the city

Bristol is the most highly qualified of all the core cities, with 42.1% of adults educated to at least degree level, while the number of residents with no qualifications is below the national average. However, this positive city-level performance disguises significant inequalities at ward level.

Office for National Statistics · the geography of a life

A decade more or less of life, depending on the ward you live in

Health inequalities within Bristol are extreme and this is thrown into sharp relief by the city's life expectancy at birth statistics. Men in some of the most deprived areas, such as Lawrence Hill and Central Bristol, are projected to die over a decade earlier than those living in least-deprived areas such as Westbury Park and Henleaze. Similarly, women in the affluent ward of Clifton Down are projected to live 9.4 years longer than those living in the highly deprived area of Filwood, less than five miles away.
